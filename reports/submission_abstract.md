# Safer, or Just More Refusals? English, Japanese and Bilingual Safety Fine-Tuning of Tiny Aya

Multilingual safety fine-tuning can reduce harmful outputs by increasing refusals, including refusals of harmless requests. We study this trade-off in Tiny Aya Water through separate experiments on restricted versus broad LoRA adaptation, training-language composition, matched cultural cues and activation-derived interventions. We post-trained adapters on English, Japanese or both languages and evaluated their replies to English- and Japanese-written harmful jailbreak prompts. Qwen3Guard labeled whether responses were Safe, Unsafe or Controversial and separately parsed refusal. “Unsafe rate” is the share of harmful prompts labeled Unsafe among parseable Safe/Unsafe responses; “over-refusal” is refusal on prompts designed as harmless twins. In the three-run language-composition study, bilingual adapters lowered judge-labeled Unsafe rates relative to English-only adapters by 2.75 percentage points on English prompts and 6.00 points on Japanese prompts. Against English×2, which repeats English rows to match bilingual training length, bilingual training had no established English-prompt advantage (−0.63 points; Holm-adjusted *p*=.112) but lowered the Japanese-prompt Unsafe rate by 7.43 points. These changes coincided with increased refusal on harmless prompts. A small earlier pilot found lower Unsafe rates for both restricted and broad LoRA than for the base model, with more harmless refusal under broad adaptation; its scale does not establish a general advantage for restricting adaptation.

In secondary per-language results, Japanese-only training had an English-prompt Unsafe rate close to English-only training (6.45% vs 6.78%; paired interval included zero) and lower refusal on English harmless twins (13.2% vs 20.4%). On Japanese-written prompts, Japanese-only had lower Unsafe rates than English-only and bilingual training, but its harmless-refusal rate was higher than English-only and slightly higher than bilingual. Thus it does not dominate both outcomes in Japanese.

In matched US–UK cue evaluations, the study detected no difference between the cues, while original US prompts differed from controlled rewrites that also changed prompt content. An initial weight-space edit reduced English harmless refusal but failed prespecified safety-preservation criteria. A later layer-18 activation-hook experiment reduced refusal on harmless twins in English and Japanese while increasing Unsafe judgments on harmful prompts in both languages. A direction-diagnostic analysis also found language information in the earlier pooled refusal direction. The refusal-decomposition figures reported in a working summary were not independently reconstructed and are not used as verified quantitative findings.

SafeTune supplied direction/neuron analysis tools for the earlier restricted-adaptation pilot; CuratorKIT generated its preference-style training pairs. AuditKit wraps Qwen3Guard in this repository’s separate local scoring interface, while the bilingual and XSTEER runs called Qwen3Guard directly. CircuitKIT work is limited to preparation and synthetic/toy integration checks; no validated Tiny Aya circuit result is claimed. All outcomes are automated judge labels, not human-validated judgments. The results show language-dependent transfer and a safety–refusal trade-off, but do not establish a safety-preserving over-refusal intervention or broad multilingual readiness.
